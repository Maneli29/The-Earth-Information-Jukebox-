Earth Jukebox: hear the oceans and the air

NASA Earth visualizations turned into sound, so you can explore them by listening. Made for NASA Space Apps 2026 (challenge: The Earth Information Jukebox).

Ready. Press play to start the sweep.

What you are hearing: sea surface temperature

  • Time is west to east. The track sweeps once around the map in about 32 seconds, starting at the International Date Line in the Pacific. Sound moves from the left speaker (west) to the right speaker (east).
  • Pitch is temperature. Low notes are cold water near 0 °C; high notes are warm water up to 32 °C. The tone also gets brighter as the water gets warmer. Notes snap to a five-note musical scale.
  • Five quick notes at each step are five bands of latitude, played from north to south.
  • Silence is land or ice. You can hear the continents as gaps.

About the data: the sound is built from one frame of a NASA visualization of sea surface currents, coloured by temperature. Temperatures are read back from the colours using the visualization's own colour legend (0 to 32 °C), so they are approximate averages, not exact measurements. It is a snapshot, not a record of change over time.

What you are hearing: air quality

  • The picture is a globe centred on the Indian Ocean, split into three vertical sections, each showing a different pollutant from a computer model. The sweep goes from the left edge of the globe to the right edge in about 32 seconds.
  • Each pollutant has its own sound. Carbon monoxide is a short, warm pluck. Fine particles (PM2.5) are a soft, sustained pad. Nitrous oxides are bell tones.
  • Higher and louder means stronger colour, which means more of that pollutant in that area. Five quick notes at each step are five bands of latitude, north to south.
  • Silence is the empty space outside the globe's curved edge.

About the data, and its limits: this frame has no colour legend, so the sound shows relative strength within each pollutant, not real concentrations. You cannot compare the loudness of one pollutant with another. The track is built from a single frame, so it is a snapshot in time.

Optional touch interface

A small Arduino device can show the same data by touch and light: a servo needle that rises with temperature, and five lights for the five latitude bands.
